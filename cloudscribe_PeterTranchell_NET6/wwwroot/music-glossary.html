This glossary defines musical terms used throughout the Peter Tranchell Foundation website and in discussions of Peter Tranchell's music. It is intended to help readers understand programme notes, catalogue descriptions, and chatbot responses about the compositions.

The definitions in this glossary draw on Open Music Theory v2 by Kyle Gullings, Chelsey Hamm, Bryn Hughes, Brian Jarvis, Megan Lavengood, and John Peterson (CC BY-SA 4.0, viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory), and the Glossary of music terminology on Wikipedia (CC BY-SA 3.0, en.wikipedia.org). Adapted for the Peter Tranchell Foundation by the site editors.

Voice Types and Vocal Forces

Soprano
the highest of the four standard voice types in choral music. In a SATB choir, sopranos sing the top melodic line. Many of Peter Tranchell's choral works feature prominent soprano parts, sometimes with solo passages.
Alto
the lower of the two female (or upper) voice types in choral music. Alto parts provide inner harmonic voices and sometimes carry melodic material. The term comes from the Italian for "high", reflecting its historical origin as the uppermost voice in early polyphony.
Tenor
the higher of the two male voice types in choral music. Tenor parts sit above basses and below altos in the choral texture. Tranchell's choral writing frequently gives tenors independent melodic lines.
Bass
the lowest of the four standard voice types in choral music. Bass parts define the harmonic foundation of a choral texture. In orchestral contexts, "bass" may also refer to the double bass.
Baritone
a male vocal range between bass and tenor. Baritone soloists appear in some of Tranchell's vocal works and are common in operatic and song repertoire.
Contralto
the lowest female singing voice type, deeper and richer than a typical alto. Contralto soloists occasionally feature in Tranchell's works for solo voice and ensemble.
Falsetto
a vocal register above the normal speaking range, used by male singers to reach notes beyond their modal (chest) voice. Sometimes used in choral and solo contexts for expressive effect.
SATB
an abbreviation for Soprano, Alto, Tenor, Bass: the four standard voice parts in a mixed choir. Most of Peter Tranchell's choral works are scored for SATB voices, sometimes with organ or orchestral accompaniment. Variants include SSA (three soprano/alto parts), SSAA (four treble parts), and TTBB (four male parts).
A cappella
singing without instrumental accompaniment, literally "in the chapel" style. Several of Tranchell's choral works are scored for unaccompanied voices, drawing on the rich tradition of English cathedral music.
Choir
a group of singers performing together. Tranchell wrote extensively for church and cathedral choirs, as well as for choral societies. His works range from small chamber ensembles to full chorus with orchestra.

Choral and Vocal Forms

Anthem
a choral composition set to a sacred text, typically performed in Anglican church services. Anthems may be for unaccompanied voices (a cappella) or with organ or orchestral accompaniment. Peter Tranchell composed numerous anthems for the chapel choirs at Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge.
Carol
a song associated with Christmas or festive occasions. Christmas carols range from simple hymn-like settings to elaborate choral compositions. Tranchell composed several carols, some of which were written for specific liturgical occasions at Caius Chapel.
Psalm
a sacred text from the Book of Psalms in the Bible, set to music for use in church services. In the Anglican tradition, psalms are chanted to specific melodic patterns called psalm tones. Tranchell contributed to the tradition of Anglican psalm-setting through the Gonville and Caius College Psalter.
Chant (Anglican)
a simple melodic formula used to sing psalms and canticles in the Anglican liturgy. Anglican chant typically uses a reciting note followed by a cadential formula, allowing the text to flow naturally. The chant index at the Peter Tranchell Foundation documents chants associated with the Caius Chapel tradition.
Canticle
a hymn or chant with a biblical text, sung as part of the liturgy. Common Anglican canticles include the Magnificat, Nunc Dimittis, and Te Deum. Tranchell set several canticles for choir and organ.
Mass
a musical setting of the liturgical text of the Eucharist (Holy Communion). The ordinary of the Mass consists of five movements: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, and Agnus Dei. Tranchell composed a Mass setting for choir and orchestra.
Motet
a choral composition, typically sacred, on a Latin or vernacular text. Motets range from simple polyphonic settings to elaborate multi-voice works. The term broadly covers unaccompanied choral works of any sacred or serious character.
Oratorio
a large-scale musical work for soloists, choir, and orchestra, typically on a sacred or dramatic text but performed without staging, costumes, or scenery. Tranchell composed oratorios including The Sending and The Peace of the City.
Cantata
a vocal composition with instrumental accompaniment, typically in several movements. The term literally means "sung" (from Italian cantare). Church cantatas are designed for liturgical use; secular cantatas are concert works.
Song cycle
a group of songs designed to be performed together as a unified set, often connected by a narrative, theme, or poetic sequence. Tranchell composed song cycles for solo voice and piano.
Art song
a composed song for solo voice and piano (or other accompaniment), typically setting a literary poem to music. The German Lied is the best-known tradition of art song. Tranchell composed art songs setting English texts.
Recitative
a style of vocal delivery that mimics the rhythms of speech, used in opera, oratorio, and cantata to advance the narrative between arias and choruses. Recitative may be accompanied by sparse instrumentation (recitativo secco) or by fuller orchestral support (recitativo accompagnato).
Aria
a self-contained piece for solo voice, usually with orchestral accompaniment, expressing a particular emotion or thought. Arias are a central feature of opera, oratorio, and cantata. In contrast to recitative, arias have a defined musical structure and are often strophic or da capo in form.

Compositional Techniques

Counterpoint
the art of combining two or more independent melodic lines simultaneously. Good counterpoint creates a texture in which each voice is melodically interesting on its own while harmonising with the others. Tranchell's choral writing frequently employs contrapuntal techniques, particularly in his anthems and motets.
Polyphony
a musical texture in which two or more independent melodic lines occur simultaneously. Polyphonic writing is characteristic of Renaissance and Baroque music and remains an important technique in choral composition. The opposite of monophony (a single unaccompanied line) and homophony (melody with chordal accompaniment).
Homophony
a musical texture in which a single melodic line is supported by chordal accompaniment, or in which all voices move together in the same rhythm. Much of Tranchell's choral writing uses homophonic textures for clarity of text-setting, interspersed with contrapuntal passages.
Imitation
a compositional technique in which a melody introduced in one voice is repeated or echoed in another voice, often at a different pitch level. Imitation is the basis of canons and fugues. Tranchell uses imitative entries in many of his choral works.
Fugue
a compositional technique in which a short melodic theme (the subject) is introduced in one voice and then taken up by other voices in succession, with the texture built through imitation and development. A fugue is one of the most elaborate forms of contrapuntal writing. The term can also refer to a complete piece written in fugal style.
Canon
a composition in which a melody introduced in one voice is strictly imitated in another voice after a fixed time delay, like a round. The canon may be at the unison, at a different interval, in inversion, or in augmentation or diminution.
Ostinato
a short melodic, rhythmic, or harmonic pattern that repeats persistently throughout a section or the whole of a piece. Ostinatos create a sense of drive and continuity. The term literally means "stubborn" in Italian.
Pedal point
a sustained or repeated note, typically in the bass, held while the harmonies above it change. A pedal point creates tension when the sustained note is dissonant with the moving harmonies and resolution when consonance returns. Common on the dominant or tonic note.
Sequence
a melodic or harmonic pattern that is repeated at successively higher or lower pitch levels. Sequences are a common device for extending phrases and building momentum in tonal music. Diatonic sequences stay within the prevailing key; chromatic sequences may introduce accidentals.
Motif
a short musical idea, typically a melodic or rhythmic fragment, that recurs and develops throughout a composition. A motif is smaller than a theme and may be transformed, combined, or varied as the piece unfolds. Tranchell's works often feature motivic development across movements.
Theme
a recognisable melodic idea that serves as the primary material of a composition or movement. A theme may be stated, varied, developed, or combined with other themes. In sonata form, the exposition typically presents two contrasting themes.
Variation
a technique in which a theme is restated with modifications to its melody, harmony, rhythm, or texture. A set of variations consists of a theme followed by a series of increasingly elaborate transformations. Variation form was widely used by composers from the Baroque period onward.

Harmony

Harmony
the combination of simultaneously sounded musical notes to produce chords and the succession of chords in a piece. Harmony is one of the fundamental elements of Western music, alongside melody, rhythm, and texture. Tranchell's harmonic language is primarily tonal with modal inflections.
Chord
three or more notes sounded simultaneously. Chords are built from intervals and are described by their root note and quality (major, minor, diminished, augmented). Chords provide the harmonic foundation for melody and texture.
Triad
a chord of three notes consisting of a root, a third, and a fifth. The four types of triad are major (bright), minor (darker), diminished (tense), and augmented (unstable). Triads are the basic building blocks of tonal harmony.
Key
the central pitch group around which a piece of music is organised. A piece in a major key uses the notes of a major scale and tends to sound bright; a piece in a minor key uses a minor scale and tends to sound darker or more serious. Tranchell's works are typically rooted in major or minor keys, sometimes with modal elements.
Key signature
the set of sharps or flats placed at the beginning of a staff to indicate the key of a piece. For example, one sharp (F♯) indicates G major or E minor; three flats (B♭, E♭, A♭) indicate E♭ major or C minor.
Modulation
a change of key within a piece of music. Composers modulate to create contrast, tension, or structural articulation. A modulation to a closely related key (such as the dominant or relative minor) is smooth; a distant modulation creates a more dramatic effect.
Mode
a type of scale characterised by a specific pattern of intervals. The two main modes in Western music are major and minor, but the term also refers to the medieval church modes (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, and others). Tranchell occasionally employs modal inflections within his predominantly tonal language.
Diatonic
using only the notes of the prevailing key or scale, without chromatic alterations. A diatonic chord progression stays within the home key. The opposite of chromatic, which introduces notes outside the prevailing scale.
Chromatic
using notes outside the prevailing key or scale, introduced through accidentals (sharps, flats, or naturals). Chromaticism adds colour, tension, and expressive possibility. A heavily chromatic passage may temporarily obscure the sense of key.
Dissonance
a combination of notes that sounds unstable or tense, creating an expectation of resolution to a consonant chord. Dissonance is not inherently undesirable; composers use it expressively and to create forward momentum. The controlled use of dissonance is fundamental to Western harmony.
Consonance
a combination of notes that sounds stable, restful, and resolved. Consonant intervals include the unison, octave, fifth, and major and minor thirds and sixths. Consonance and dissonance work together to create harmonic tension and release.
Cadence
a harmonic formula that marks the end of a phrase or section, creating a sense of arrival or pause. The most common cadences are the authentic cadence (V to I, sounding conclusive) and the half cadence (ending on V, sounding incomplete). Tranchell uses cadences to articulate the structure of his choral phrases.
Resolution
the movement from a dissonant chord to a consonant one, providing a sense of arrival and release. Resolution is the satisfying conclusion to harmonic tension and is a fundamental principle of tonal music.
Inversion
a chord in which a note other than the root is in the bass. A triad in first inversion has the third in the bass; in second inversion, the fifth. Inversions create smoother bass lines and more varied harmonic colour.
Transposition
the process of moving a passage of music to a different pitch level while preserving the intervallic relationships. A melody transposed up a perfect fifth retains its character but sounds higher. Transposition is distinct from modulation, which implies a change of key within a continuous musical context.

Melody and Intervals

Melody
a sequence of notes perceived as a single entity; a tune. Melody is the most immediately recognisable element of music and typically carries the primary musical idea. In choral music, the melody may be shared among voices or concentrated in one part.
Interval
the distance in pitch between two notes. Intervals are described by their size (second, third, fourth, etc.) and quality (major, minor, perfect, augmented, diminished). Intervals are the building blocks of melody and harmony.
Scale
a set of notes arranged in ascending or descending order by pitch, forming the raw material for melody and harmony. The two main scales in Western music are the major scale (bright, with the pattern W-W-H-W-W-W-H where W = whole step, H = half step) and the minor scale (darker, with variations including natural, harmonic, and melodic minor).
Step (conjunct motion)
movement from one note to the next note in the scale, i.e. an interval of a second. Stepwise motion produces smooth, flowing melodies. The opposite of leap (disjunct motion).
Leap (disjunct motion)
movement between notes that are more than a second apart. Leaps create drama and energy in a melody but are typically followed by stepwise motion in the opposite direction to restore balance.
Passing tone
a non-chord note that fills the gap between two chord tones, moving by step in a single direction. Passing tones create smooth melodic connections and are among the most common types of ornamental (non-harmonic) note.
Neighbour tone
a non-chord note that moves by step away from a chord tone and then returns to the same chord tone. Neighbour tones ornament a melody by briefly departing from and returning to a stable pitch.
Appoggiatura
a non-chord note that is approached by leap and resolves by step, typically on a strong beat. Appoggiaturas create expressive dissonance and are a hallmark of lyrical vocal writing.
Suspension
a non-chord note held over (suspended) from a previous chord into a new chord, creating a dissonance that then resolves downward by step. Suspensions are a powerful expressive device, creating tension and release. Common suspension types include the 4–3, 7–6, and 9–8 suspensions.

Rhythm and Metre

Rhythm
the pattern of durations of notes and silences in music. Rhythm gives music its sense of movement and is inseparable from melody and harmony in actual performance.
Metre
the regular pattern of strong and weak beats in music, indicated by the time signature. Common simple metres include 3/4 (waltz time) and 4/4 (common time). Compound metres include 6/8 and 9/8, where each beat subdivides into three rather than two.
Time signature
the fraction-like symbol at the beginning of a piece indicating the metre. The top number shows how many beats per bar; the bottom number shows the note value that receives one beat. For example, 3/4 means three crotchet beats per bar; 4/4 (common time) means four crotchet beats.
Tempo
the speed at which music is performed, typically indicated by a metronome marking (e.g. ♩ = 120) or by an Italian term. Common tempo markings include allegro (fast, lively), andante (walking pace), adagio (slow), and presto (very fast).
Ritardando (rit.)
gradually slowing down the tempo. A ritardando is often used at the end of a phrase or section to create a sense of winding down. The opposite of accelerando.
Accelerando (accel.)
gradually increasing the tempo. An accelerando creates excitement and momentum. The opposite of ritardando.
Rubato
a flexible approach to tempo in which the performer slightly speeds up and slows down for expressive effect, while maintaining the overall pulse. Rubato literally means "robbed time" in Italian — the performer borrows time from one passage and returns it elsewhere.
Fermata
a pause or hold, indicated by a curved line with a dot over a note or rest (𝄐). The performer holds the note for longer than its written value, typically at the discretion of the conductor or performer. Fermatas often appear at the end of movements or before significant structural moments.
Syncopation
a rhythmic effect in which emphasis is placed on normally weak beats or off-beats, creating a sense of surprise or propulsion. Syncopation is common in jazz, popular music, and many 20th-century classical styles.
Pickup (anacrusis)
one or more notes that precede the first full bar of a piece or phrase. A pickup note (or upbeat) falls before the downbeat and helps establish momentum into the opening bar.

Dynamics and Expression

Dynamics
the relative volume at which music is performed. Dynamics range from very soft (pianissimo, pp) to very loud (fortissimo), with gradations including piano (p, soft), mezzo-piano (mp, moderately soft), mezzo-forte (mf, moderately loud), and forte (f, loud). Composers use dynamic markings to shape phrases and create contrast.
Crescendo (cresc.)
gradually getting louder. A crescendo builds tension and excitement and is one of the most fundamental expressive devices in music. Indicated by a hairpin symbol opening to the right, or by the word crescendo.
Diminuendo (dim.) / Decrescendo (decresc.)
gradually getting softer. A diminuendo releases tension and can create a sense of fading or withdrawal. The opposite of crescendo. Also called decrescendo.
Piano (p)
soft. A dynamic marking indicating that the passage should be performed quietly. Pianissimo (pp) means very soft; mezzo-piano (mp) means moderately soft.
Forte (f)
loud. A dynamic marking indicating that the passage should be performed with strength and volume. Fortissimo (ff) means very loud; mezzo-forte (mf) means moderately loud.
Sforzando (sfz)
a sudden, sharp accent on a single note or chord. Sforzando indicates a forceful attack followed by a rapid decrease in volume, creating a striking emphasis.
Dolce
sweetly. An expressive direction indicating that a passage should be performed with gentle, tender feeling. Common in lyrical vocal and instrumental music.
Cantabile
in a singing style. An instruction to perform a passage with the phrasing and expressiveness of vocal music, even on an instrument. Cantabile passages are typically lyrical and flowing.
Espressivo
expressively. A direction to perform with feeling and emotional engagement. The marking is subjective and invites the performer to bring personal interpretation to the passage.
Legato
smoothly connected, with no gaps between notes. Legato is the opposite of staccato (detached). In vocal music, legato reflects the natural flow of the voice; in instrumental music, it is achieved through sustained bowing, fingering, or breath control.
Staccato
detached, with each note shortened and separated from the next. Staccato creates a crisp, pointed effect and contrasts with legato. Indicated by a dot above or below the note head.

Musical Form

Form
the structure or layout of a piece of music, defined by how its sections are organised, contrasted, and related. Common forms include binary (AB), ternary (ABA), sonata form, theme and variations, and rondo. Form gives music its large-scale sense of shape and direction.
Movement
a self-contained section of a larger work, such as a symphony, sonata, or suite. Each movement typically has its own tempo, key, and character. Tranchell's orchestral and chamber works are often in multiple movements.
Exposition
the opening section of a piece in sonata form, in which the principal themes are presented, typically in the home key and a contrasting key. The exposition establishes the material that will be developed and recapitulated later.
Development
the middle section of sonata form, in which the themes from the exposition are fragmented, combined, modulated, and transformed. The development section is typically the most harmonically and contrapuntally complex part of a sonata-form movement.
Recapitulation
the return of the exposition material in the final section of sonata form, now presented primarily in the home key. The recapitulation provides resolution and closure after the harmonic instability of the development.
Coda
a concluding section appended to the end of a movement or piece, providing finality and resolution. A coda may introduce new material or revisit existing themes in a summarising or valedictory manner. The term literally means "tail" in Italian.
Da capo
"from the head"; an instruction to return to the beginning and repeat. A da capo aria in Baroque opera follows an ABA structure, with the singer returning to the opening section (often with ornamentation). Tranchell occasionally uses return-to-beginning structures in his vocal works.
Strophic form
a form in which the same music is repeated for each stanza or verse of a text. Strophic form is common in hymns, folk songs, and settings of multi-verse poems. It prioritises the text and allows the listener to focus on the words.
Rondo
a form in which a principal theme (refrain) alternes with one or more contrasting episodes (ABACA or similar). The recurring refrain creates a sense of return and unity, while the episodes provide variety.
Ternary form (ABA)
a three-part form consisting of a principal section (A), a contrasting section (B), and a return of the first section (A'). Ternary form is one of the most common structures in Western music, found in minuets, arias, and many individual movements.
Binary form (AB)
a two-part form in which the first section (A) establishes the key and the second section (B) provides contrast before either ending in a different key or returning to the home key. Binary form was standard in Baroque dance movements.

Orchestration and Instruments

Orchestration
the art of assigning musical material to the various instruments of an orchestra, and the technique of writing effectively for orchestral forces. Good orchestration exploits the distinctive timbres and capabilities of each instrument. Tranchell was a skilled orchestrator, writing for full orchestra, chamber ensembles, and various solo combinations.
Timbre
the distinctive quality or colour of a sound that distinguishes one instrument or voice from another, even when playing the same pitch. Timbre is determined by the harmonic content (overtones) of a sound and is one of the most important elements of musical expression.
Organ
a large keyboard instrument in which air is forced through pipes to produce sound. The organ has a vast range of tonal colours achievable through different stops (sets of pipes). Church organs are central to the Anglican liturgical tradition in which Tranchell worked, and organ features prominently in his choral and solo works.
Organ stop
a device on the organ that selects a particular set of pipes, each producing a distinct timbre. Stops are named by their character (e.g. Diapason, Flute, Reed) and pitch (e.g. 8' = concert pitch, 4' = an octave higher, 16' = an octave lower). The combination of stops determines the organ's sound at any moment.
Registration
the choice and combination of organ stops used to produce a particular sound. Registration is an expressive decision that affects the colour, volume, and character of the music. Organists select registrations appropriate to the style, mood, and dynamic level of each passage.
Strings
the family of bowed string instruments in an orchestra: first violins, second violins, violas, cellos, and double basses. The string section forms the core of the orchestra and is the most versatile in terms of range, dynamics, and expressive capability. Tranchell scored several works for string orchestra or string ensemble.
Woodwinds
the family of wind instruments originally made of wood, including flutes, oboes, clarinets, and bassoons. Woodwinds offer a wide range of timbres and are used both for solo passages and for enriching the orchestral texture.
Brass
the family of instruments made of brass, including trumpets, horns, trombones, and tuba. Brass instruments produce powerful, resonant sounds and are used for heroic, solemn, or festive effects in orchestral music.
Percussion
instruments that are struck, shaken, or scraped to produce sound, including timpani (kettledrums), snare drum, bass drum, cymbals, and many others. Percussion provides rhythmic drive, colour, and dramatic emphasis.
Chamber music
music composed for a small ensemble of instruments, typically one player per part. Common chamber groups include string quartets (two violins, viola, cello), piano trios, and wind quintets. Chamber music is more intimate than orchestral music, with each player carrying an independent part. Tranchell composed chamber works for various ensemble types.

Sacred Music and Liturgy

Liturgy
the prescribed form of public worship in a church. In the Anglican tradition, the main liturgical services are the Eucharist (Holy Communion), Morning Prayer (Mattins), and Evening Prayer (Evensong). Much of Tranchell's choral music was composed for use in the liturgy at Gonville and Caius College Chapel.
Evensong
an Anglican evening prayer service, typically sung by a cathedral or college choir. The service includes psalms, canticles (usually the Magnificat and Nunc Dimittis), and an anthem. Choral Evensong is a cornerstone of the English cathedral tradition in which Tranchell's music is performed.
Anthems
(see under "Choral and Vocal Forms" above). In the Anglican context, the anthem is sung after the Evening Prayer or at other points in the liturgy, as distinct from the Roman Catholic motet.
Introit
a short anthem or psalm sung at the opening of a church service, particularly at the Eucharist. Introits set a specific liturgical text and establish the mood of the service. Tranchell composed introits for the Caius Chapel choir.
Offertory
a piece of music performed during the offertory, the part of the Eucharist in which the bread and wine are brought to the altar and the congregation's offerings are collected. Offertory sentences are short scriptural texts set to music.
Communion anthem
a choral piece performed during the distribution of Holy Communion. Communion anthems are typically reflective and subdued in character, matching the solemnity of the moment.
Processional
a hymn or anthem sung during the entrance (procession) or exit (recession) of clergy and choir at a church service. Processional music is typically stately and rhythmically regular, suited to walking pace.
Plainchant (Gregorian chant)
the monophonic, unaccompanied vocal music of the medieval Western Church, sung in Latin. Plainchant uses free rhythm (not governed by a regular beat) and a limited range of modes. It forms the oldest continuous musical tradition in Western Christianity and has influenced composers across the centuries.
Polychoral
a style of composition involving two or more choirs, often positioned in different parts of a building, singing in alternation or together. Polychoral writing creates spatial effects and antiphonal contrasts. The term is particularly associated with Venetian music of the late Renaissance and Baroque periods.

Pitch and Notation

Clef
a symbol placed at the beginning of a musical staff to indicate the pitches of the notes. The treble clef (G clef) is used for higher-pitched instruments and voices; the bass clef (F clef) for lower ones; the alto and tenor C clefs for middle-range voices and instruments such as the viola and cello.
Accidental
a sharp (♯), flat (♭), or natural (♮) placed before a note to indicate that it is raised or lowered by a semitone from the note prescribed by the key signature. Accidentals apply to the note they precede and to all subsequent notes in the same bar unless cancelled.
Octave
the interval between one pitch and another with half or double its frequency. Notes separated by an octave have the same letter name and sound essentially the same, differing only in register. The octave is a fundamental concept in musical pitch organisation.
Pitch class
all notes that share the same letter name regardless of octave. For example, all C's (C3, C4, C5, etc.) belong to the same pitch class. Pitch class is a useful concept for analysing harmonic and melodic patterns independent of specific register.

Musical Genres and Tranchell's Output

Concerto
a composition for one or more solo instruments and orchestra, typically in three movements. The concerto showcases the soloist's virtuosity in contrast with the larger ensemble. Tranchell composed concertos including a Piano Concerto and a Concerto for Oboe and Strings.
Symphony
a large-scale orchestral work, typically in four movements, ranging from the Classical era to the present day. The symphony is one of the most ambitious forms in Western music. Tranchell composed symphonies for full orchestra.
Suite
a collection of short instrumental pieces, originally associated with dance forms. Suites may be drawn from ballet, theatre, or stage music, or composed as independent concert works. Each movement typically has its own tempo and character.
Partita
a type of suite, particularly for solo instrument, consisting of a series of dance movements. The term was commonly used in the Baroque period and was revived by some 20th-century composers.
Sonata
a composition for one or two instruments, typically in three or four movements. A sonata for piano is one of the most common types; a sonata for violin and piano is a standard chamber work. Tranchell composed sonatas for various instruments.
Interlude
a short piece played between larger sections of a service, play, or longer musical work. Interludes may be reflective, dramatic, or decorative in character. Tranchell composed organ interludes for use in the liturgy.
Choral society
a large amateur choir that performs major choral works in concert, as distinct from a cathedral or college chapel choir. Choral societies typically rehearse weekly and perform works requiring large forces, such as oratorios, masses, and cantatas. Tranchell's larger choral works were performed by choral societies.
Premiere
the first public performance of a composition. The premiere of a new work is a significant event, often attended by critics and the musical community. Several of Tranchell's works received their premieres at Caius Chapel or in Cambridge concert venues.

Related Abbreviations and Conventions

Op. / Opus
a number assigned to a composition to indicate its place in a composer's output, from the Latin for "work". Composers typically assign opus numbers in order of composition or publication. Tranchell's opus numbers are used in the Foundation's thematic catalogue.
MS / MSS
manuscript / manuscripts. Refers to hand-written scores, as distinct from published (printed) editions. Several of Tranchell's works survive only in manuscript, held in the Cambridge University Library collection.
CUL
Cambridge University Library, where the Peter Tranchell archive is deposited. Manuscript classmarks beginning with "MS.Tranchell" identify specific items in the collection.
Duration
the total playing time of a musical work, typically given in minutes and seconds. Duration is a practical consideration for concert programming and is listed in the Foundation's catalogue for each work.
Forces
the instrumental and/or vocal forces required to perform a work. Forces are specified in the catalogue and may include voice types (SATB, solo), instruments (organ, piano, strings), and ensemble type (string quartet, orchestra).
Precis
a brief summary of the musical content, character, and context of a work, provided in the Foundation's catalogue for each entry. Precises are written to help performers, programmers, and listeners understand the nature and background of each composition.
